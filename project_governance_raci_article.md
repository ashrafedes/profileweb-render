# Project Governance and the RACI Matrix: Defining Roles, Authority, and Structured Communication

![Project governance meeting with stakeholders reviewing a RACI matrix on screen](https://images.pexels.com/photos/3184291/pexels-photo-3184291.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

*A clear governance framework turns confusion into coordinated execution.*

Project governance is the system by which an organization directs, controls, and monitors its projects. It defines who makes decisions, how information flows, what standards apply, and how accountability is enforced. Without governance, projects drift, stakeholders pull in different directions, and decisions are made informally — often by the loudest voice in the room rather than by the most qualified person.

At the operational heart of governance sits the **RACI matrix**. RACI stands for **Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed**. It is a simple, powerful tool that translates governance principles into day-to-day project execution. When used correctly, the RACI matrix removes ambiguity about who does what, prevents tasks from falling through the cracks, and reduces the communication overload that plagues complex projects.

This article explores how project governance and the RACI matrix work together, provides a step-by-step method for building a RACI matrix, examines common mistakes, and offers advanced techniques for applying RACI in large, cross-functional, and agile environments.

---

## What Is Project Governance?

Project governance is the set of structures, processes, and rules that ensure projects align with organizational strategy and deliver value. It is broader than project management. While project management focuses on planning, executing, and closing a specific project, governance ensures that the project is the right one, is funded appropriately, and is governed by clear authority and reporting lines.

### The Three Pillars of Project Governance

Every governance framework rests on three pillars:

1. **Authority** — who has the power to approve scope, budget, schedule, and major changes.
2. **Accountability** — who is answerable for outcomes and decisions.
3. **Communication** — how information is shared among stakeholders, teams, and decision-makers.

When these pillars are weak, projects suffer from:

- **Decision paralysis**: nobody knows who can approve a change.
- **Scope creep**: changes are approved informally without impact analysis.
- **Rework**: teams receive conflicting instructions from multiple sources.
- **Blame games**: when things go wrong, accountability is unclear.

A strong governance framework answers four fundamental questions for every project:

- Who owns the project outcomes?
- Who decides what, and at what level?
- Who needs to be consulted before a decision?
- Who must be told after a decision is made?

The RACI matrix answers these questions at the task level, making governance tangible and actionable.

## What Is a RACI Matrix?

A RACI matrix is a responsibility assignment chart that maps tasks, decisions, or deliverables against the people or roles involved in a project. For each activity, it assigns one of four roles:

- **R — Responsible**: The person or people who perform the work.
- **A — Accountable**: The person who owns the result and has final decision-making authority. Only one person can be Accountable for each task.
- **C — Consulted**: People whose opinions are sought before a decision or action. Consultation is typically two-way communication.
- **I — Informed**: People who need to be kept up to date but do not provide input. Communication is one-way.

The power of RACI lies in its simplicity. It forces the project manager and sponsor to think explicitly about every task: who acts, who owns, who advises, and who listens. This clarity prevents the overlapping responsibilities, gaps, and communication bottlenecks that derail projects.

## The Four RACI Roles in Detail

Each letter in RACI has a specific meaning and a specific place in the project workflow. Misunderstanding these roles is one of the most common reasons RACI matrices fail.

### Responsible (R)

The Responsible role is assigned to the person or team that actually does the work. Multiple people can be Responsible for a single task, especially when a task requires collaboration.

**Example**: For the task "Prepare project schedule," the Responsible parties might be the project planner and the project manager. For "Execute site testing," the Responsible party might be the quality engineer and the testing crew.

### Accountable (A)

The Accountable person is the one who answers for the result. They have the final say and are answerable if the task is late, incomplete, or incorrect. There can only be one Accountable person per task. If two people are Accountable, neither is.

**Example**: The project manager is Accountable for the project schedule, even if a planner does the actual work. The head of engineering is Accountable for the design approval, even if senior engineers prepared it.

### Consulted (C)

Consulted parties are those whose expertise or perspective is needed before a decision is made. Consultation is not endless deliberation; it is targeted input at the right time. Too many Consulted parties slow decisions down; too few lead to uninformed decisions.

**Example**: Before finalizing a construction schedule, the project manager might Consult the procurement lead to confirm material availability and the safety officer to confirm labor restrictions.

### Informed (I)

Informed parties need to know the outcome but do not participate in the decision. The flow is one-way. Keeping the right people informed prevents surprises and maintains stakeholder confidence.

**Example**: After a milestone is approved, the project sponsor and the PMO director are Informed. After a change request is approved, the finance team and the legal team are Informed.

## How Project Governance and RACI Work Together

Project governance defines the rules of the game. The RACI matrix puts those rules on the field. Governance without a RACI matrix remains abstract and unenforceable. A RACI matrix without governance becomes a bureaucratic chart with no backing authority.

### RACI Brings Governance Down to the Task Level

Governance documents such as project charters, steering committee terms of reference, and stage-gate policies define high-level authority. The RACI matrix translates that authority into specific tasks. It tells every team member:

- What am I expected to do?
- What do I have the authority to decide?
- Whose input do I need before acting?
- Whom must I notify after I act?

### Governance Gives RACI Enforcement Power

A RACI matrix is only useful if the organization respects it. That respect comes from governance. If the governance framework says the project sponsor is the escalation point for unresolved ownership disputes, then RACI conflicts can be resolved quickly and consistently.

For example, if a task has two people marked Accountable, governance defines the process for resolving the conflict: the project manager decides, or the matter is escalated to the steering committee. Without that governance backing, RACI becomes a debate rather than a standard.

## Step-by-Step Guide to Creating a RACI Matrix

Building an effective RACI matrix requires more than filling in a spreadsheet. The following workflow ensures the matrix reflects real project responsibilities and gains team buy-in.

### Step 1: Identify the Work Breakdown Structure Activities

Start with the project's work breakdown structure (WBS) or a task list. The activities should be granular enough to assign clearly but not so detailed that the matrix becomes unmanageable. Aim for 15 to 50 rows for most projects.

### Step 2: List the Stakeholders and Roles

Across the top of the matrix, list the people or roles involved. Use roles rather than names when possible, so the matrix remains valid even if people change. Typical roles include:

- Project Sponsor
- Project Manager
- Workstream Lead
- Technical Architect
- Procurement Lead
- Quality Manager
- Safety Officer
- PMO / Governance Office

### Step 3: Assign One Accountable Person per Task

For each task, first assign the Accountable (A). This is the most important step. The Accountable person is the owner of the result. If you cannot identify a single owner, the task is not ready to be assigned.

### Step 4: Assign Responsible, Consulted, and Informed Roles

Once the owner is clear, identify who will do the work, whose input is needed, and who must be kept informed. Be disciplined: only Consult people whose input genuinely changes the quality of the decision. Only Inform people who need to act on the outcome or report on it.

### Step 5: Validate with the Team

A RACI matrix created in isolation will be ignored. Review it in a workshop with the people named in it. Ask them:

- Is this task actually in our scope?
- Is the Accountable person the right one?
- Are we missing any Consulted parties?
- Are Informed parties overloaded?

### Step 6: Approve and Publish

Embed the approved RACI matrix in the project management plan, the communication plan, and the project governance framework. Make it accessible to everyone. Update it when the project structure, scope, or team changes.

### Sample RACI Matrix for a Construction Project

| Activity | Project Sponsor | Project Manager | Engineering Lead | Procurement Lead | Contractor | Safety Officer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Approve project budget | A | R | C | C | I | I |
| Develop detailed design | I | A | R | C | C | I |
| Issue tender documents | I | A | C | R | C | I |
| Award subcontract | A | R | C | R | C | I |
| Conduct weekly safety inspection | I | C | I | I | R | A |
| Approve change request | A | R | C | C | C | I |
| Report monthly progress to board | I | R | I | I | I | I |

In this example, the Project Sponsor is Accountable for budget and change approval, the Project Manager is Accountable for execution and reporting, and the Safety Officer is Accountable for safety inspections. Each task has exactly one A.

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

RACI matrices are simple in concept but easy to misuse. The following mistakes appear repeatedly in organizations that treat RACI as a checkbox exercise.

### Mistake 1: Multiple Accountable People

When two people are Accountable for the same task, neither is. The result is slow decisions and diluted ownership. The rule is strict: one A per task.

**Solution**: If two people seem equally responsible, split the task into two sub-tasks, each with one owner, or define a decision-making hierarchy.

### Mistake 2: Everyone Is Consulted

Over-consultation paralyzes decision-making. If ten people are Consulted on every task, nothing moves.

**Solution**: Limit Consulted parties to those whose expertise, authority, or support is genuinely needed. Use the rule: "Would this person's input change the decision?"

### Mistake 3: Nobody Is Accountable

A task with several Responsible people but no Accountable person often fails because no one owns the outcome.

**Solution**: Always start by assigning the A before assigning the R.

### Mistake 4: Confusing Responsible and Accountable

Responsible people do the work; Accountable people own the result. In small teams, one person may be both R and A, but the distinction still matters.

**Solution**: On a matrix, make sure R and A are separate columns so the distinction is visible.

### Mistake 5: Creating the Matrix Too Late

A RACI matrix created mid-project still has value, but it cannot undo confusion that has already occurred.

**Solution**: Develop the RACI matrix during project initiation or as part of the project kickoff workshop.

### Mistake 6: Never Updating It

People change, scope evolves, and governance needs shift. A stale RACI matrix becomes a source of errors.

**Solution**: Review the RACI matrix at stage gates, after major changes, and during lessons-learned reviews.

## RACI in Different Project Contexts

The RACI matrix is flexible enough to adapt to different project methodologies and organizational structures. However, the way it is applied should reflect the context.

### RACI in Predictive (Waterfall) Projects

In predictive environments, the RACI matrix is typically detailed and linked to the WBS. Each work package has clear R, A, C, and I assignments. Governance is formal, with stage gates and steering committees.

### RACI in Agile Projects

Agile teams often resist formal RACI matrices because of their emphasis on self-organization. However, governance does not disappear in Agile; it is just distributed differently. A lighter RACI matrix can define:

- Who is Accountable for the product backlog?
- Who is Accountable for sprint goals?
- Who is Consulted on architectural decisions?
- Who is Informed about releases?

The matrix should be lean and avoid prescribing daily work that the team already self-manages.

### RACI in Matrix Organizations

In matrix organizations, team members report to both functional managers and project managers. RACI is essential to clarify whether the functional manager or the project manager is Accountable for a task. Without this clarity, matrix structures create chronic conflict.

### RACI in Multi-Contractor Construction Projects

Large construction projects involve owners, main contractors, subcontractors, consultants, and regulators. A RACI matrix that spans organizational boundaries prevents disputes over who approves design changes, who is responsible for inspections, and who must be informed of delays.

## Advanced Considerations

For experienced practitioners, several advanced techniques can extend the value of RACI beyond the basics.

### RACI-VS

Some organizations add two more roles:

- **V — Verifies**: the person who checks that the work meets standards.
- **S — Signs Off**: the person who formally approves completion.

This is useful in regulated industries or projects with strict quality gates, such as pharmaceuticals, aviation, and infrastructure.

### RASCI

Another common extension is the **S — Support** role. Support assists the Responsible person but is not the primary doer. This is helpful when a senior expert guides a junior analyst.

### RACI and Decision Rights

Advanced governance frameworks combine RACI with explicit decision rights:

- **Recommend**: who proposes the option.
- **Agree**: who must consent for the decision to proceed.
- **Perform**: who executes the decision.
- **Input**: who provides data or advice.
- **Decide**: who has final authority.

This RAPID model is more complex than RACI and is typically used at the executive or portfolio level.

### RACI and Communication

The C and I roles in RACI are the foundation of the project communication plan. If the matrix is accurate, the communication plan almost writes itself. Each Consulted party needs a two-way channel; each Informed party needs a one-way channel. The frequency, format, and owner of each communication can be derived from the RACI matrix.

## FAQ

**Can a person be both Responsible and Accountable?**

Yes, especially in small teams. However, as projects grow, it is better to separate the roles to create clear ownership and review layers.

**How many Consulted parties should one task have?**

Ideally no more than two or three. More than that usually indicates the task needs to be split or the decision process needs redesign.

**Is RACI only for large projects?**

No. Even a small project with three people benefits from a short RACI discussion. The format can be simple, but the clarity is universal.

**How often should the RACI matrix be updated?**

At minimum at phase gates and after any major change in scope, team, or governance. In fast-moving projects, a monthly review is appropriate.

**What happens if the team ignores the RACI matrix?**

The matrix is then only a document. The project manager and sponsor must enforce it by referencing it in meetings, escalations, and performance reviews.

## Conclusion

Project governance defines how authority, accountability, and communication operate in a project. The RACI matrix is the operational tool that makes governance visible and enforceable at the task level. Together, they remove ambiguity, accelerate decisions, and prevent the conflicts that arise when roles are unclear.

A well-built RACI matrix is not a bureaucratic formality. It is a living document that answers the questions people ask every day: Who does this? Who decides? Who do I ask? Who needs to know?

For project managers, the next step is simple: at the start of your next project, build the RACI matrix before building the Gantt chart. When roles are clear, schedules are easier to keep, changes are easier to manage, and project teams are far more likely to succeed.
