Why Fiber Network Projects Fail Before the First Cable Is Installed

By Ashraf Ibrahim El Desoky · Sep 28, 2026 · 14 min read

A fiber project rarely fails on the day someone finds a damaged cable. Failure usually starts much earlier—when a survey is accepted without sufficient verification, when a design does not reflect the field, when a permit is treated as a formality, when procurement is disconnected from sequencing, or when several contractors work against different definitions of “complete.” By the time the symptom appears in the field, the organization is often dealing with consequences rather than causes.

I have seen this repeatedly across more than twenty‑five years in telecommunications, fiber infrastructure and large implementation environments: OSP expansion, FTTH/FTTx, complex multi‑contractor programmes, and portfolio‑level delivery in Saudi Arabia. The cable is visible. The management conditions that determine whether that cable becomes a working network are often invisible. That is where projects are won or lost.

The Fiber Is Usually Not the Hardest Part

From an engineering perspective, fiber is mature. Routes can be designed, ducts installed, splicing performed, OTDR testing completed, network elements commissioned. Yet large deployments remain surprisingly difficult. The reason is simple: the fiber is only one component of a larger delivery system involving designers, surveyors, civil contractors, fiber contractors, subcontractors, suppliers, testing teams, municipalities, utility authorities, telecom operators, clients and PMO/project controls. Each can perform reasonably well and the overall project can still slip—because the project is not the sum of activities but the interaction between them.

A civil section can finish “on time” while access is still restricted. Material can be in the warehouse while the workfront is not ready. A route can be approved on drawings yet become impossible because site conditions changed. Reported physical progress can look high while much of the work remains untested or unaccepted. These do not always indicate weak capability. They indicate the programme is losing synchronization—and synchronization is the hardest thing to achieve at scale.

The Problem Begins with Assumptions

Every plan rests on assumptions. Surveys assume access. Designers assume constructability. Procurement assumes arrival dates. Contractors assume released workfronts. Clients assume approval cycles. Schedules assume dependencies behave. Most assumptions are reasonable in isolation; danger emerges when several interact. A two‑week civil delay that feeds splicing, testing, acceptance and service activation does not create “one delay”—it creates a dependency cascade. Experienced leaders therefore look beyond activities to relationships; a smaller package with heavy dependencies can be riskier than a larger, self‑contained package.

A Master Schedule Is Not Enough

Large programmes often have impressive schedules: thousands of activities, multiple WBS layers, baselines and dashboards. They can still fail to control delivery if reality contains relationships the model does not represent. A permit can drive construction without a sufficiently detailed activity. A material issue can affect several workfronts at once. Productivity decline is visible in production rates long before a milestone slips. A design revision can ripple through procurement, construction and testing before the master file is formally updated. The most valuable question is not “what is late?” but “which changing conditions can make future activities late?” That shift moves the organization from reaction to anticipation.

Progress Percentage Can Mislead

Two projects show 85% completion. One has repetitive, well‑understood remaining work, approvals in place and testing capacity. The other has the hard 15%: difficult crossings, unresolved permits, constrained workfronts, brittle interfaces, incomplete documentation and commissioning dependencies. Both read 85%. They are not equally healthy. The closer a project gets to completion, the more risk concentrates in integration and acceptance. Numbers are not necessarily wrong; interpretation is incomplete.

The Contractor Is Not the Delivery System

Contractor performance cannot be separated from interface performance. “Ready to install” is meaningless if the route is not released, drawings are under review, access is denied or permits are pending. Every statement can be technically accurate and the programme still cannot move. The goal is not only to make each contractor meet its contract; it is to make the entire chain move together. That requires clarity on where responsibility starts and ends, where dependencies live, who owns each unresolved interface and what happens if it remains unresolved.

The Route on the Drawing Is Not the Route in the Field

A drawing represents planned conditions; the field is a living environment. Utilities shift. Access changes. Other crews occupy corridors. Authorities add restrictions. Records differ from reality. Design that is technically correct may not remain executable. Field verification cannot be a formality. On the New Administrative Capital project, surveying, civil works, shop‑drawing review, material inspection, documentation and consultant coordination moved together; accuracy mattered, but continuous feedback from reality mattered more. Mature delivery does not ask only whether the design is correct; it asks whether it is still executable now. That is a higher standard.

Quality Is Cheaper to Create Than to Recover

Quality is not a ceremony at the end. Cable handling, bending radius, storage, splicing discipline, route installation, testing and documentation are progressive quality. Final acceptance discovers what execution created weeks earlier. The later a defect is found, the more expensive the recovery. OTDR, insertion‑loss and continuity tests are not paperwork; they are the mechanisms by which physical work proves it produced the intended technical result. Verification is not the end of control—it is part of control.

The Most Dangerous Delay Is the Delay Nobody Owns

Issues are normal. What becomes dangerous is an issue discussed for weeks without becoming an owned decision. Reports repeat it. Meetings repeat it. Nothing changes. Governance must be more than visibility; it must allocate ownership, a decision path and a consequence for inaction. The useful question is not “is this still open?” but “what prevents closure, and who has authority to remove that constraint?” That single shift turns meetings from recitals of information into engines of removal.

When the PMO Becomes Strategic

A PMO that only consolidates reports is a missed opportunity. Portfolio‑scale delivery needs portfolio‑level intelligence: pattern detection across projects, recurring contractor issues, systemic permit constraints, material problems affecting multiple fronts, productivity trends and risks that are invisible when projects are viewed separately. In large Saudi programmes, scale can turn a small repeated problem into a strategic one. This is where standards, assurance, integrated visibility and delivery accountability need a coherent operating model—see the PMO excellence framework.

From Project Controls to Project Intelligence

Modern programmes generate huge amounts of data—schedules, progress, materials, testing, permits, drawings, RFIs, reports, resources, finances. The opportunity is to connect the signals so the system recognizes patterns early: three periods of declining productivity, rising material consumption, accumulating approvals, and remaining work concentrating on few fronts. No single indicator proves failure; together they form a trend. Technology helps only when tied to sound processes and evidence—see earned value management as a control signal when it is tied to physical acceptance rules.

The Difference Between Laying Fiber and Delivering a Network

Installing cable is not the same as delivering a network. Success is not only kilometres installed. It is infrastructure that is accepted, documented, integrated and capable of supporting the intended service. The question is not “how much did we install?” but “how much usable, accepted and operational infrastructure did we create?” That measure changes conversations, priorities and contracts.

Experience Changes the Questions

Experience does not make projects easy; it makes warning signals visible. You notice when a schedule is becoming fragile, when minor issues begin to connect, when busy crews are not productive, when high reported progress hides a hard finish, when an unresolved interface is a future commissioning problem. The value of controls is not the report they produce but the decision they enable. Those questions lead to different decisions—and different outcomes.

The Real Challenge—and the Answer

FTTH and FTTx will scale. 5G will demand reliable backhaul and access. Scale increases contractors, interfaces, approvals, dependencies—and the chance for small problems to become systemic. The answer is not heavier management; it is smarter management. A project should know what is happening, why it is happening, what it will affect and what decision is needed before the problem becomes expensive. That is the purpose of control.

Closing Thought

The cable is rarely the hardest part. The harder challenge is creating an environment in which hundreds of people, activities, approvals, materials, systems and decisions move toward one outcome without losing synchronization. That requires planning that understands dependencies, controls that understand trajectory, governance that produces decisions, contractors managed as one delivery system, quality built into execution and leadership that recognises problems early enough to influence them. Ultimately, the objective is not to install more fiber—it is to turn investment into a controlled, measurable, operational result.
