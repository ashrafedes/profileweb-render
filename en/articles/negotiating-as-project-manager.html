Why Project Managers Are Negotiators Whether They Know It or Not

By Ashraf Ibrahim El Desoky· Aug 7, 2026· 4 min read

You Negotiate More Than You Think

A project manager spends between 70 and 90 percent of the working day communicating, and a large share of that communication is negotiation in disguise. Every scope change, every resource request, every deadline discussion is a negotiation — whether or not anyone at the table calls it one. Most project managers never make this connection. They picture negotiation as something that happens in a boardroom with lawyers and contracts, when in reality it happens in corridor conversations, progress meetings, and email threads about variations.

Where the Negotiation Actually Happens

A subcontractor asking for a time extension is a negotiation. A client requesting extra scope "while you are already there" is a negotiation. Your best engineer being pulled onto another project is a negotiation with the functional manager. A sponsor demanding an earlier date with no additional budget is perhaps the hardest negotiation of all. During my years on national telecom rollouts, I negotiated daily — with municipalities over permits, with subcontractors over rates and crew sequencing, with landowners over site access, and with my own management over resources. None of these appeared on the organisation chart as "negotiation," yet every one of them decided whether the programme hit its dates.

The Cost of Negotiating Badly

A project manager who does not recognise a negotiation gives value away by default. He accepts scope creep to appear cooperative, agrees to a compressed schedule to avoid an awkward conversation, or concedes a rate without asking for anything in return. Each concession feels small in the moment; the cumulative effect is margin erosion, team burnout, and a programme that slowly drifts out of control. The fix is not becoming combative — it is becoming deliberate. Enter each discussion knowing your best alternative, the other side's real interest, and what you can afford to trade. A "yes" that receives something in return is delivery; a "yes" that receives nothing is leakage.

Practical Habits That Work

Preparation beats persuasion. Before any significant discussion, know your walk-away position and your ideal outcome — most negotiators lose the deal before the meeting even starts because they never decided what they needed. Separate the person from the problem: you can be completely firm on the issue and entirely warm on the relationship at the same time, and the best programme negotiators do exactly that. Finally, always close the loop in writing. Verbal agreements on site evaporate within a week; a two-line confirmation email becomes your institutional memory and your protection when memories later differ.

You are already negotiating every day — on scope, on time, on money, on people. The only real question is whether you are doing it on purpose.
